The Caesar family is defined by geometry. Where a crop's fringe is textured and graduated, a Caesar's is cut straight across — a level, horizontal line drawn on the forehead — and the top is a consistent length rather than tapered from back to front.

The style appears on Roman portraiture two thousand years old and has never left circulation, which is a fair argument for its durability. It is also one of the most useful shapes for covering an uneven hairline, a widow's peak or a high forehead, because a blunt line hides everything above it.

The **Caesar cut** is the classic version, worn over short tapered sides with minimal product. The **Edgar cut** takes the same idea to a graphic extreme: the fringe line is cut hard and low, and the sides drop into a high skin fade with no softening between the two.

Both are unforgiving of growth. A level line across your forehead makes a centimetre obvious, and a fringe-only trim between full cuts is worth asking about.
