The Edgar takes the Caesar's straight fringe and pushes every element of it toward maximum contrast. The fringe line is cut hard and level across the forehead. The sides are taken to a high skin fade. Between the two there is no gradient, no softening and no ambiguity.

It is a genuinely graphic haircut — closer to a piece of design than most cuts get — and it has become one of the defining shapes of the past few years. It is also, by some distance, the most demanding cut here.

## Who it suits

Oblong and long faces are the natural home, for the same reason the Caesar is: a hard horizontal line across the forehead shortens the face decisively. Square faces get an aggressive graphic pairing. Diamond faces benefit from the tight sides at the cheekbones.

Oval faces can carry it. Round faces should approach carefully — the horizontal fringe adds width to a wide face, and the high fade's narrowing effect only partly offsets it.

Straight and thick hair produce the cleanest line. Coily hair carries it well and the contrast against faded skin is superb, though the fringe edge will read as slightly softer. Very fine hair struggles: a hard line needs density behind it to look solid rather than sparse.

## Getting the cut right

Both elements need specifying. "Hard straight line across the front, high skin fade on the sides" covers it. Some barbers will soften the fringe by instinct — a soft-edged Edgar is just a French crop, so if you want the hard version you have to say so.

Fringe height matters enormously. The line usually sits low, close to the eyebrows, which is part of what makes it graphic. Ask where they intend to put it before they start.

## Styling it day to day

Comb the fringe forward and flat. Anything that introduces lift at the front works against the line. A small amount of firm wax along the fringe edge keeps it reading as a defined line rather than a soft edge.

Keep product away from the faded sides. Bare scalp with product on it looks greasy in a way that short hair with product on it does not.

## Living with it

Ten days, honestly. Both defining features fail on roughly the same schedule: the fringe line loses its edge as the ends grow unevenly, and the skin fade develops visible stubble at the base.

There is no relaxed version of this cut's maintenance. If a fortnightly barber visit is not something you will actually do, the French crop over a mid taper gives you a related look on a four-week cycle, and it is the honest recommendation for most people.
