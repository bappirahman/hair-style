Before clippers dominated barbershops, this was simply what a haircut looked like. The classic taper is cut scissor-over-comb, which means the sides shorten gradually with no discrete guard steps anywhere in the blend. There is no line, because no line was ever drawn.

The look is longer and softer than any clipper cut. Weight is kept on the sides, the perimeter is tidied rather than removed, and the whole thing is usually finished with a side parting. It reads as traditional rather than trend-driven — which, as fades have become the default request everywhere, has made it quietly distinctive again.

## Who it suits

Square and oval faces are the natural home. The retained side weight balances a strong jaw rather than exaggerating it. Oblong faces do notably well, because a classic taper adds horizontal volume at the sides — the opposite of what a high fade does — and shortens the apparent length of the face.

Heart-shaped faces work with the parting positioned to break up the forehead. Round faces are the weakest fit: keeping width at the sides is the last thing a round face needs, and a taper fade would serve better.

Fine hair is well served, because nothing is exposed and the scissor blend keeps every available strand contributing to the shape. Thick and wavy hair suit it too, though thick hair needs weight removed internally or the sides will bulk out.

## Getting the cut right

Ask for scissors specifically. "Classic scissor taper, no clippers on the sides" is unambiguous. If a barber reaches for clippers over comb instead, that is a reasonable substitute for the same effect, but the finished blend should still have no visible steps.

Discuss the parting. A natural parting — wherever your hair already wants to split — always sits better than an imposed one. Ask your barber to find it while your hair is wet and cut to it.

## Styling it day to day

This is a combed cut, and it wants to be treated as one. Comb the parting in while the hair is damp, then dry along the line of the part so it sets cleanly. Finger-styling a classic taper produces something that looks like a different, messier haircut.

A little shine is appropriate here in a way it is not on modern textured cuts. A light pomade or a cream with slight gloss suits the traditional shape. Heavy matte clay fights it.

## Living with it

Six to eight weeks, with no awkward stage whatsoever. Because there is no line and no exposed scalp, the cut simply gets longer. Many people find it looks best at week three or four, once the freshly cut edges have softened.

The neckline is the only thing that dates it, and a quick tidy between visits keeps the whole cut looking deliberate for considerably longer than the schedule suggests.
