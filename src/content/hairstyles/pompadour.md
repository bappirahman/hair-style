The pompadour is the most sculptural haircut in common circulation. Hair at the front is left long, swept upward from the hairline, and rolled back over itself into a rounded mass of volume above the forehead. The sides are kept short so nothing competes with it.

It has been through several distinct eras — eighteenth-century court, 1950s rock and roll, the 2010s revival — and each has interpreted the height differently. What has not changed is the underlying requirement: this shape is built, not worn. There is no version that happens by itself.

## Who it suits

Round faces gain enormously. Vertical height above the forehead is the most direct available correction for a face that reads wide, and no cut delivers more of it. Square faces get a striking classic pairing. Heart faces do well because the volume balances a narrow chin.

Oval faces carry it comfortably. Oblong and long faces should be cautious — adding several centimetres of apparent height to an already long face is rarely flattering, and a lower quiff serves better.

Thick straight hair is the requirement, more or less. The shape depends on hair standing up under its own weight with product helping rather than doing all the work. Wavy hair works and gives a softer, more textured pompadour. Fine hair can achieve a small version with volume powder and diligent drying, but the daily effort is significant. Curly hair does not suit this shape without straightening.

## Getting the cut right

The critical instruction is graduation. The front should be longest and the length should reduce toward the crown, so the volume has somewhere to fold back into. A pompadour cut at a single length on top produces a mound rather than a sweep.

Keep the sides tapered rather than faded to skin unless you specifically want the modern high-contrast version. A classic pompadour sits over short sides with some weight left in them.

## Styling it day to day

The order matters more than the products. Volume powder or a thickening spray on damp roots first — this is where the height actually comes from. Then dry with a round brush, lifting the front section up and rolling it backward, working in sections.

Only once the shape is dry and standing does pomade come in, and it goes on the surface, not into the roots. Working product down to the scalp at this point collapses everything the dryer built. Ten minutes is a realistic morning.

## Living with it

The cut lasts four to six weeks — the sides need tidying before the top does. What the pompadour actually demands is daily, not weekly. There is genuinely no wash-and-go version of this shape.

If that sounds like too much, the modern pompadour is shorter, sits lower and takes about half the time, while still reading unmistakably as the same family.
