A high fade takes the sides down close all the way to the parietal ridge, the bony corner where the side of your skull turns into the top. Above that line the hair is left long; below it, it disappears into skin. The result is the highest contrast a fade can produce while still technically being a blend rather than a disconnect.

That contrast is the entire appeal, and it is also the entire commitment. A high fade is a cut that looks deliberate from thirty feet away. It reshapes the perceived proportions of the head more than any other short back and sides, making the face read narrower and taller.

## Who it suits

Round faces gain the most. Stripping width from the sides and stacking visible height on top directly counteracts the two things that make a round face read round. Heart and diamond shapes also do well, because the tight sides draw the eye up and away from a narrow chin or prominent cheekbones.

Oval faces can carry it comfortably. The one shape to be careful with is oblong — a high fade adds vertical emphasis to a face that already has plenty, and the effect can be unbalancing. If your face is long and you want the contrast, ask for a mid fade with a hard line instead; you get most of the graphic quality without the added height.

Thick, curly and coily hair are the natural partners. Dense hair on top against faded skin is the classic pairing, and coily hair in particular holds a shape above the fade that straight hair needs product to achieve. Fine hair is the difficult case: a high fade exposes a lot of skin, and if the top cannot hold volume the cut collapses into something that reads as thinning rather than sharp.

## Getting the cut right

Two decisions define the outcome. First, where the fade tops out — this should follow the ridge, which curves down toward the back of the head. A high fade cut on a level ring rather than a curve produces a distinctly artificial, helmet-like edge at the crown.

Second, whether the top of the fade is hard or blurred. A hard line gives you the graphic version and needs re-cutting every two weeks to stay convincing. A blurred, or "skin-to-scissor," transition softens over four weeks instead of collapsing, and is the version most people are happier living with.

Ask for the temple corners to be squared rather than rounded if you want the shape to look intentional. Rounded corners on a high fade tend to soften the whole effect.

## Styling it day to day

Because the sides contribute nothing, everything depends on the top section holding its shape. Dry the hair with the dryer pointed in the direction you want it to sit — back for a swept look, forward for a crop. Then layer product: a cream for pliable shape first, a small amount of clay or fibre over the top for grip.

Resist the temptation to over-apply. The faded sides are bare skin, and any product that migrates down onto them reads immediately as grease.

## Living with it

Ten days to two weeks. That is the honest lifespan of a high fade at its best. The shortest section regrows fastest in proportional terms, so the bottom of the gradient fills in first and the contrast starts flattening from the ear upward.

At three weeks a high fade looks like an untidy mid fade. There is no graceful decline. If a two-week barber cycle is not realistic for you, this is the wrong cut, and a mid fade with a hard line will get you eighty percent of the look on double the schedule.
