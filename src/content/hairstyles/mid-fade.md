If the low fade is discreet and the high fade is emphatic, the mid fade is the one that gets chosen most often — because it does the useful part of both. The taper begins around the temple, roughly level with the outer corner of the eyebrow, which puts the contrast squarely in the visible front three-quarter view rather than hiding it behind the ear.

That placement matters more than it sounds. A fade you can only see in profile is a detail. A fade that starts at the temple changes the perceived width of your head, and it is the reason a mid fade makes almost everyone look a little sharper in the jaw.

## Who it suits

This is the most face-shape-agnostic fade in the catalogue. On a round face, taking the sides down at temple height removes horizontal width exactly where a round face has too much of it, and the length left on top adds the vertical it needs. On a square face it does the opposite job equally well — it exposes the strong bone structure rather than burying it.

Oval faces can wear any fade, so the mid is simply the safe middle. Heart-shaped faces do well provided some weight is retained just above the fade line, otherwise the forehead can look wide relative to a very tight temple.

Hair type is barely a constraint. Straight hair gives the cleanest gradient; wavy and curly hair give a more interesting one. Thick hair is the natural fit, because a mid fade is partly a weight-removal exercise. Very fine hair is the one case where a low fade is usually the better call — a mid fade can expose more scalp above the ear than fine hair can comfortably cover.

## Getting the cut right

The transition on a mid fade should sit halfway between the top of the ear and the parietal ridge — the bony corner where the side of your skull turns into the top. If the fade climbs above that ridge, you have a high fade, whether or not that is what you asked for. Barbers vary in where they read that line, so it is worth pointing at your own temple when you ask.

The second thing worth specifying is whether you want the fade to be *hard* or *blurry* at the top. A hard line looks graphic and photographs well; a blurry one grows out invisibly. Most people are happier with blurry, and most barbers default to hard if you don't say.

## Styling it day to day

The top section is doing all the work, so it wants texture rather than hold. Rough-dry the hair with your fingers, working against the direction of growth, then apply a clay to dry or nearly dry hair — clay on wet hair goes gummy and drags the roots flat.

Push the front up and slightly forward, then break the whole thing apart with dry fingers. The finished look should have visible separation between sections. A mid fade with a solid, unbroken top reads as a helmet no matter how good the fade underneath is.

## Living with it

Three weeks is the realistic window. The fade itself will still be defined at week two and pleasantly soft at week three. Past that, the shortest section grows into the section above it, the gradient flattens, and the sides start to push outward rather than tapering in.

Because the transition sits in plain view, a mid fade tells on you faster than a low one when it is overdue. If you can only get to the barber monthly, ask for the fade a touch lower than you actually want — it will pass through your ideal shape somewhere around week two.
